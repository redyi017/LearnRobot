## Core thesis (2026-10-08)
AI is advancing very fast in the digital world; the big opportunity is AI that interacts with the physical world.

## Question: why do so many companies build humanoids?

My intuition: it looks like a vague target. Notes to test against later reading:

**Arguments companies make for humanoids**
- The world is built for human bodies (stairs, doors, tools, workstations), so in theory one form factor can go anywhere without redesigning the site.
- **Data:** there are huge amounts of human video and human motion, and a human-shaped robot can (in principle) learn from it and be teleoperated by a person more naturally.
- Generality: if robot foundation models work, one body plus one model across many tasks could give software-like economics.
- Capital: the humanoid story raises very large rounds, and funding is itself a strategic asset.

**Arguments against (my skepticism)**
- Legs are expensive, power-hungry, and a safety problem (a falling 60 kg robot). Most factory and warehouse floors are flat, where wheels win.
- Most paying jobs need 1–2 arms, a gripper, and reliability, not a human silhouette.
- Many real deployments of "humanoid" companies use wheeled bases or stationary torsos.
- Generality is a bet on future models; customers pay for one job done reliably today.

**My open question:** is the humanoid a product, or a data-collection and fundraising strategy? → revisit in Modules 4–5 (foundation models, humanoids).

## Interest: soft robotics
Saw a demo of a gripper made of many connected honeycomb/hive-shaped cells grasping objects stably.

Notes so far:
- Soft and compliant grippers handle fragile, irregular objects (food, produce, packages) without precise perception or force control. The *material* does part of the control work ("morphological computation").
- Related designs to look up: Fin Ray effect fingers, pneumatic soft grippers, granular jamming grippers, origami/lattice grippers.
- Trade-offs: lower payload and precision, durability and wear, harder to model and simulate.
- Possible wedge: soft end-effectors are a *component* business. You can sell grippers to people who already own arms, which is far less capital-intensive than building a whole robot.
